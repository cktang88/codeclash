# Rout and recovery: what really happened, and rules for Code Clash

Research for the game designer (Templars, Samurai, Undead). Question: how did routs start and spread, what did fugitives do, and how, how often and under what conditions did routed troops rally?

## Method and honesty note

- Sources were fetched in this session (Wikipedia battle articles used as leads, plus search summaries). Each claim below carries the URL it came from.
- Items marked **[background]** come from general historiographic knowledge (Keegan, Delbrück, du Picq, Oman, Verbruggen, Turnbull) and were **not** re-verified online here. Treat them as leads to check before quoting.
- Items marked **[unverified]** were searched for and not found. The main one is the verbatim Templar Rule article text; see section 3.1.
- Hard percentages for "what fraction of a broken force rallied" do not exist in the sources. The numbers in "Game rules suggested" are design estimates anchored on the cases, and are labelled as such.
- Wikipedia-sourced casualty figures are contested and often derive from chroniclers who exaggerated. They are used for order of magnitude only.

## 1. How routs started and spread

### 1.1 Triggers seen in the cases

| Trigger | Case | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Rumour the commander is dead | Hastings 1066: the Norman left fled when a rumour spread that William had been killed | Wikipedia: "A rumour started that William had been killed, which added to the confusion." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings ; English Heritage says the left flank fled on the rumour and some English pursued downhill https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/1066-and-the-norman-conquest/what-happened-battle-hastings/ |
| One wing breaks first, then contagion to the neighbour | Hastings: the retreat was blamed on the Breton division on William's left, described as the start of the general retreat | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings |
| Missile attrition plus thirst plus encirclement | Hattin 1187: the Frankish infantry "fled en masse" onto the Horns of Hattin and played no further part | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hattin |
| Fugitives collide with the formation behind | Crécy 1346: Genoese crossbowmen were routed early and the following French knights hacked at them, adding to disorder in the first charge | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cr%C3%A9cy |
| Horses panic into friendly infantry | Agincourt 1415: wounded horses "galloped through the advancing infantry, scattering them and trampling them down" (Barker, as quoted on Wikipedia) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Agincourt |
| Uncommitted rear/reserve flees when the front dies | Agincourt: the rearguard was largely valets holding horses and leaderless; Keegan argues the uncommitted reserves "fled the field" | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Agincourt |
| Death or flight of the high command | Bannockburn 1314: panic spread through the English remnants after Gloucester's death; Edward II was led off by Pembroke, and the rout became general | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bannockburn |
| Treachery or defection | Sekigahara 1600: Kobayakawa Hideaki's 15,000 turned on Otani's flank, other commanders (Wakisaka, Ogawa, Akaza, Kutsuki) switched, the Western line was rolled up from its right | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sekigahara |
| Retreat cut off, forced assault, then breaking under firepower | Nagashino 1575: Takeda attacked in waves "blocked by matchlock guns" and fences; by midday "the Takeda forces began their rout" | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nagashino |
| Pursuit that disorders the pursuers, then counter-hit | Hastings, Lewes, Arsuf, Edgehill (see section 6) | see below |
| Prolonged fatigue, darkness, loss of the king | Crécy: Philip left near midnight "and most of the army melted away"; the next morning late arrivals were routed and pursued for miles | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cr%C3%A9cy |

### 1.2 Who breaks first

- **Rear and flank first, front last [background].** Keegan (The Face of Battle) and du Picq both stress that panic starts away from the contact line, among men who are idle and cannot see. A Keegan retrospective notes du Picq saw the value of multiple Roman lines as keeping rear soldiers away from front-line panic, and that the Imperial Guard rout at Waterloo began at the rear of the column. https://acoup.blog/2023/03/31/michael-taylor-on-john-keegans-the-face-of-battle-a-retrospective/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Face_of_Battle
- **Last to flee die most.** An (admittedly non-scholarly) forum summary: a unit that holds to the end takes only a few percent; a unit that breaks takes much more, and the men who flee last are most likely to be killed. https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/the-fate-of-routing-units.1035265/latest . Treat as anecdotal; it matches Keegan [background].
- **Weakest elements go first:** Hattin infantry, Crécy Genoese, Agincourt valets and the baggage-guarding rearguard, Bannockburn infantry. Elite mounted men-at-arms were the last to go (Hattin: knights and mounted serjeants "though disorganized, kept fighting" through three charges). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hattin
- **Flank threats and encirclement** produce the largest breakages: Hattin, Nagashino, Sekigahara.

### 1.3 Where casualties were taken: melee versus pursuit

- Generic: a rout is a "panicked, disorderly and undisciplined retreat"; light cavalry, auxiliaries and militia are effective pursuers who can keep chasing into the next day, "causing the routed army heavy casualties or total dissolution." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rout
- The standard claim in military history is that the great majority of a defeated pre-gunpowder army's deaths came after it broke, in the pursuit [background: Delbrück, Goldsworthy, Sabin]. A targeted search did not return a citable percentage (Sabin, Lost Battles, https://www.historyofwar.org/bookpage/sabin_lost_battles.html, models this but the excerpt did not state a figure). **[unverified as a number]**
- Case figures:
  - Bannockburn: Reese (via Wikipedia) estimates about 11,000 of 16,000 English infantry were killed (about 69 percent); Walsingham gives about 700 men-at-arms. Many were killed "by the pursuing Scots or by local people along the route to the border"; only one sizeable group (Welsh spearmen under Maurice de Berkeley) escaped intact. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bannockburn
  - Nagashino: Takeda about 10,000 killed (two thirds of the besieging force, per Shincho Koki), against about 6,000 for Oda-Tokugawa. Most senior commanders died in the withdrawal rather than in the main fighting, and Nobunaga "pounced on them when their ranks gave way." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nagashino
  - Hastings: Marren speculates about 2,000 Normans and 4,000 English dead; the English side includes the Malfosse and hillock rally actions in the final collapse. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings
  - Crécy: English dead possibly 40 to 300; French noble dead 1,542 counted by heralds; Burne's estimate about 12,000 French dead total. The lopsided ratios imply most French dead fell in the repeated charges and the night/next-morning pursuit. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cr%C3%A9cy
  - Agincourt: French about 6,000 killed (mostly nobles), 700 to 2,200 captured; English 112 to 600. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Agincourt
- Rule of thumb for design: winners lose a few percent; losers lose roughly 10 to 30 percent in the fighting and, when pursuit is effective (cavalry-rich pursuer, no terrain refuge, hostile countryside), another 20 to 60 percent of the army killed or captured afterward. Bannockburn and Nagashino sit at the top of that range; Arsuf (below) at the bottom, because the pursuit was deliberately halted.

## 2. What fugitives did

### 2.1 Distance, scatter and clumping

- **Foot fled to the nearest refuge or terrain, in clumps, not across the open.** Hattin: infantry went up the Horns of Hattin (a hill, a short distance, then trapped). Arsuf: Ayyubid troops fled into woodland and hills ("A third charge scattered the Ayyubids into the woodland and hills."). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hattin ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arsuf
- **Mounted fugitives ran far, in loose groups, towards friendly strongpoints.** Edward II fled with about 500 men to Stirling (refused entry), then Dunbar, then by ship to Berwick; nobles took refuge at Bothwell Castle and later surrendered. Mikatagahara: Ieyasu reached Hamamatsu Castle with five men. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bannockburn ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mikatagahara
- **Castle as sump:** fugitives with a fortified place within a day's ride were usually able to be gathered; those without were hunted down in the countryside (Bannockburn, with local people killing stragglers).
- **Distance figures in the cases:** Lewes: Edward's Londoners fled and were chased about 4 miles (6 km) toward Offham. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Lewes . Arsuf: the first pursuit halted after about 1.5 km. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arsuf . Crécy: next-morning rout "pursued for miles".
- **Throwing away arms:** the best verified item here is a 19th century description of a rout (First Bull Run) in which men dropped packs, blankets and muskets and officers' threats failed against "a cruel, crazy, mad, hopeless panic." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rout . For medieval cases the sources fetched do not say directly; the shield, spear and heavy armour were commonly dropped or cut away by foot [background: Oman, Verbruggen]. Mounted knights generally kept their weapons and armour on, but lost horses (Arsuf Hospitallers lost mounts; unhorsed knights in armour could not escape, which is why Hattin and Agincourt produced so many captives).

### 2.2 Cavalry versus foot fugitives

| | Cavalry fugitives | Foot fugitives |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Fast; can outrun foot pursuit, not fresh cavalry | Slow; die to any horse |
| Cohesion | Come off in small retinue groups tied to a lord (500 with Edward II) | Break into individuals; clump on terrain |
| Survival | Higher if horse sound; captured if unhorsed | Low in the open (Bannockburn about 69 percent dead) |
| Refuge | Castle, camp, friendly column | Hill, wood, marsh, river (Bannockburn burn) |
| Rally | Practical within a few km if the lord survives | Rare unless a solid body (reserve, laager) sits behind them |

### 2.3 Fugitives as a hazard to their own side

Hattin infantry deserting; Crécy Genoese hacked down by French knights; Agincourt horses trampling the advancing French foot; Edgehill fugitives from Essex's brigade distracting Lucas and Grandison as they tried to rally 200 Royalist horse. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Edgehill . Rule implication: a rout wave should push/disorder friendly units it passes through.

## 3. Rallying in practice

### 3.1 The Templar rule (banner)

What was actually found:

- Wikipedia's Baucent article, citing Schottmuller (Der Untergang des Templer-Ordens, 1887, pp. 74-78) who cites Munter (Statutenbuch des Ordens der Tempelherren, 1794), says:
  - A brother separated from his banner "was obliged to try to reach the nearest Christian banner in the field."
  - "No brother was permitted, on pain of expulsion from the order, to leave the field of battle" while "at least one banner of the order was still flying."
  - If all Templar banners were lost, "the men were expected to flock to the nearest banner of the Hospitallers" (or any other Christian banner still flying).
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baucent
- Also reported by a search summary of OSMTH material: only when the last standard was lost might a Templar flee. https://carpediem.osmth.org/carpe_diem_2021_spr02.htm (the page itself did not quote the Rule; the full article PDF is https://carpediem.osmth.org/documents/2021_spr_doc01.pdf, not read).
- Banner: white gonfanon with a black chief, per Jacques de Vitry (c. 1220), colours said to symbolise harshness to enemies and kindness to friends. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baucent
- Squadron (eschielle) banners: each squadron had its own, per Wikipedia/Munter paraphrase.

**[unverified]** The verbatim Rule text and article numbers were NOT located. The authoritative English text is J. M. Upton-Ward, The Rule of the Templars: The French Text of the Rule of the Order of the Knights Templar (Boydell, 1992), in the "Retrais" section on conduct in battle. Search result listing: https://backstory.london/products/9780851157016 . Do not quote article numbers until someone checks the book. The designer's remembered wording (rally to the piebald banner or nearest Christian banner; do not leave the field while a banner stands) is consistent with the secondary paraphrase above.

Designer note: the same Rule also forbids breaking ranks to pursue without leave of the commander (the marshal controlled the charge and the turcopoles/squires screened). I could not fetch the verbatim text; see section 6.3 for the Arsuf evidence that discipline was an ideal, not always the practice.

### 3.2 Hastings 1066 (Norman rally; feigned flight)

- A rumour that William was dead spread; "William rode through his forces, showing his face and yelling that he was still alive" and counter-attacked. Some English rallied on a hillock but were overwhelmed. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings
- The pursuing English left their flanks exposed. Gravett: a brisk Norman counter-attack "proved to be the turning point of the battle." Historians debate whether the pursuit was ordered by Harold or spontaneous. Same source.
- William of Poitiers (Norman partisan) has William lifting his helmet; the Bayeux Tapestry shows it; one caution is that Poitiers may have turned a real retreat into a feint. https://penelope.uchicago.edu/~grout/encyclopaedia_romana/Britannia/anglo-saxon/hastings/william.html (Chicago translation hosted by Penelope; text not fetched in full)
- Feigned flights: "Most historians agree it was used at Hastings"; effect was to thin the housecarl line, replaced by fyrd levies. Rearguard at the Malfosse: some English seriously wounded Eustace of Boulogne before being killed. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings
- Lessons: (a) rally was triggered by the commander's visible person and voice, within seconds to minutes and a few hundred metres; (b) the rallied troops immediately counter-attacked the disordered pursuers; (c) the pursuers, not the fugitives, were the ones who lost cohesion.

### 3.3 Arsuf 1191 (both sides)

- Hospitaller rearguard under attack; Garnier de Nablus asked repeatedly to charge and was told to wait for six trumpet blasts; the marshal and Baldwin le Carron charged with "St. George!" and the rest followed. Richard converted it to a general charge. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arsuf ; also https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garnier_de_Nablus ; https://www.britannica.com/event/Battle-of-Arsuf
- Ayyubid side rallied: Taqi al-Din rallied about 700 of the Sultan's bodyguard and hit Richard's left flank; Richard charged again and they broke again. Baha al-Din found only 17 bodyguards and a drummer with Saladin during the rout. After, Saladin regrouped but would not risk a full-scale attack. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arsuf
- Richard halted the first pursuit after about 1.5 km, regrouped, charged a second time, regrouped, charged a third time; some Ayyubids "turned on the knights who had charged too far ahead" (James d'Avesnes killed). Same source.
- Lesson: the rally was done by an elite household unit (about 700 bodyguards) around a commander, at a short distance, and was beaten again when it met formed charging heavy cavalry. The Crusader victory came from re-forming between charges (command control by the king), not from a free pursuit.

### 3.4 Hattin 1187

- Infantry fled to the Horns and took no further part. Raymond of Tripoli's two charges: the second broke through to Lake Tiberias and he escaped. Guy's knights and mounted serjeants "though disorganized, kept fighting"; three failed charges; the army broke. Only about 200 knights escaped; about 200 captured Templar and Hospitaller knights were beheaded; captured infantry enslaved. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hattin
- Lesson: a broken force that is encircled, dehydrated and without refuge does not rally; the elite who rally and break out are the exception (an escape of about 200 knights is a small fraction of the field army). The military orders died at the banner rather than flee, then were executed: the discipline had a price.

### 3.5 Bannockburn 1314

- Panic spread to the remaining English and became a rout; Edward fled with about 500; Pembroke and Giles d'Argentan seized the reins; d'Argentan turned back and died. The Earl of Gloucester charged and was killed. Welsh spearmen under Berkeley made it to Carlisle intact. A party of nobles, including the Earl of Hereford, took refuge in Bothwell Castle. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Bannockburn
- Lesson: no rally of note on the field; the survivors who kept formation (a disciplined Welsh spear block) survived best. Rally to a castle happened, but as shelter, not as a return to the fight.

### 3.6 Crécy 1346

- Series of French charges "continued late into the night"; historians disagree on the number (the commonly quoted figure is about 15 or 16 [background]). Each fresh formation moved to the foot of the hill before attacking; later charges crossed ground "strewn with dead horses and men." The sources say little about deliberate rallying. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Cr%C3%A9cy
- Interpretation: "re-forming for repeated charges" was mostly new units arriving piecemeal, and surviving cavalry reforming individually at the foot of the slope, not a rout-and-rally cycle. Failed charges bled strength each time, and the cumulative result was a night rout.
- The Bohemian king's tie-bridles with attendants and plunge shows the opposite of a rally: a knightly "no retreat" bond; all died.

### 3.7 Agincourt 1415

- French cavalry failed, horses fled through their own infantry; the second line was swallowed up; the leaderless rearguard of valets did not form a rally point; Keegan argues the uncommitted reserves fled when Henry ordered the prisoners killed because he feared a French regroup. Peasants and men-at-arms attacked the English baggage. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Agincourt
- Lesson: the threat of a rally (and the sight of still-formed French on the field) triggered the killing of prisoners. A rallying enemy mass is tactically meaningful. Nothing rallied in practice.

### 3.8 Edgehill 1642 (early modern comparison)

- Royalist cavalry routed Parliament's wings and chased to Kineton, stopping to loot the baggage; Lucas and Grandison rallied about 200 men but were distracted by Essex's brigade fugitives; some Royalist horse returned late. The Parliamentarian reserve cavalry (Stapleton, Balfour) emerged through gaps in the foot and put many Royalist infantry to flight. Ruthven rallied the Royalist infantry after the King's sons were moved to safety; Welch recaptured the Royal Standard. Casualties about 500 killed and 1,500 wounded each side. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Edgehill
- Lesson: a reserve left uncommitted behind the line is the classic recovery tool; the victor's cavalry took hours to come back, by which time the battle was a draw.

### 3.9 Sengoku Japan

- **Mikatagahara 1572/3:** After the second Takeda attack, a two-pronged cavalry charge followed by foot drove Tokugawa into disorderly retreat. Ieyasu fled; Natsume Yoshinobu led a suicide charge to cover him. Ieyasu ordered Okubo Tadayo to plant his golden fan standard (uma-jirushi) on a hill and rally the troops toward the castle town. At Hamamatsu he returned with only five men, ordered gates open and braziers lit to guide returning troops; Sakai Tadatsugu beat a big drum. The Takeda vanguard (Baba Nobuharu, Yamagata Masakage) read this as a trap and halted; about 100 foot and 16 gunners raided the Takeda camp that night; Shingen withdrew. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mikatagahara ; also https://samurai-archives.com/wiki/Battle_of_Mikatagahara
- This is the best documented samurai rally mechanism found: a **standard on high ground, a fortified gate, lights and a drum**, with a bluff that stopped pursuit. Casualty figures are disputed (infobox: Takeda 500 to 3,000).
- **Nagashino 1575:** Takeda retreat was cut off by a detachment (Sakai Tadatsugu), forcing an assault; repeated waves failed; the rout began by midday; the pursuit killed most senior commanders. No rally occurred, because the pursuer was pushing and the army's best men (the Twenty-Four Generals) were dying in rearguard. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nagashino
- **Sekigahara 1600:** the west collapsed through defections (Kobayakawa, Wakisaka, Ogawa, Akaza, Kutsuki) and Ukita's own vassals deserting; Otani died and his troops retreated; Mori and Kikkawa stayed out. Shimazu Yoshihiro cut a breakthrough retreat through the Tokugawa lines and arrived with about 200 men; Ii Naomasa was wounded pursuing. Konishi, Ishida and Ankokuji were captured and executed on 6 November. Casualty estimates range 8,000 to 35,000. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Sekigahara
- Lesson: lord-centred loyalty. Retainers rallied round the lord's banner (uma-jirushi, sashimono, nobori) because their fate and fief depended on him. Where the lord was dead or defected (Otani, Ukita), the retinue dissolved. The Shimazu "advance to retreat" (sutegamari-like, rear men sacrificed) is the model for a disciplined fighting withdrawal [background: Turnbull].
- I did not find primary or secondary text that specifically describes samurai rallying on a banner other than Mikatagahara. The role of banners in Sengoku command is in Turnbull, Samurai (cited by the Rout article's reference list, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rout). **[background]**

### 3.10 Rallying points: reserve, camp, laager, commander

- **Reserve:** Edgehill Parliamentarian cavalry reserve; Lewes baronial reserve defeated Henry's unsupported assault https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Lewes ; Arsuf Richard's reformed charge groups.
- **Fortified place:** Hamamatsu gate, Bothwell, Dunbar, Stirling (refused).
- **Wagon laager/baggage:** worked as a stand for Hussites and others [background: Taborite wagenburg]; in our cases the baggage was a target, not a rally point (Edgehill, Agincourt). A baggage raid in the rear can cause the stand-fast reserve to break (Agincourt prisoners order).
- **Commander in person:** Hastings (William), Mikatagahara (Ieyasu's retreat and Okubo's standard), Arsuf (Richard, Taqi al-Din). The presence of the lord was the most common rally trigger in every case with a surviving rally.

### 3.11 Who rallied: elite versus levy

| Group | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Household cavalry and bodyguards | Arsuf, 700 bodyguards under Taqi al-Din; Hattin knights and serjeants fought on in disorder; Mikatagahara Tokugawa retainers |
| Retinues of knights with a lord | Edward II's 500; Bothwell nobles |
| Disciplined spearmen, close order | Berkeley's Welsh at Bannockburn |
| Levy infantry | Hattin infantry (no rally), Bannockburn infantry (about 69 percent killed), Hastings fyrd at the hillock (rallied but overwhelmed) |
| Missile troops and mercenaries | Genoese at Crécy; broken early and cut down |

Distance: effective rally in the cases happened within about 0.5 to 2 km (Arsuf's 1.5 km halt; Hastings within sight of the army), unless the fugitives reached a fortification. Beyond 5 km (Lewes' Londoners, 6 km) the group was effectively gone for the battle.

## 4. Joining another unit

- **Templar rule:** explicitly allowed (and required) to join the nearest Christian banner, then the Hospitallers if all Templar banners were lost. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baucent . This is cross-unit, cross-order rally, but it is an organised order rule, not a general medieval practice.
- **General practice:** knightly retinues re-formed on whichever lord was nearby; mixed groups of men-at-arms formed ad hoc around a prominent banner. Edgehill: Lucas and Grandison rallied about 200 horse out of broken units; Ruthven rallied infantry. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Edgehill . Hastings: the Normans rallied and fought mixed (Bretons, Normans, French) under William himself. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings
- **Samurai:** rallying was tied to one's own lord (uma-jirushi). Cross-clan mixing happened mostly in defeat when the lord was dead and men attached to a nearby friendly daimyo, but I found no cited example **[unverified]**. Okubo Tadayo's standard at Mikatagahara gathered Tokugawa retainers of several houses under an allied commander's flag, which is the nearest case. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mikatagahara
- **Fugitives of a different type:** infantry fugitives joining cavalry is rare; fugitives usually clump at a hill, wall or reserve of their own type. Knights joined a body of other knights.
- Design result: allow cross-unit joining for same-class troops and for the Templar trait; otherwise make it a weaker, slower rally (see rules).

## 5. Timescales and odds

Verified data points (all from the sources above):

- Hastings: the rumour to William's reappearance and counter-attack was effectively immediate (minutes). The feigned flights were repeated over hours. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings
- Arsuf: from the Ayyubid break, a rally of about 700 bodyguards occurred in the first phase, and Richard needed two regroupings (each probably tens of minutes) between three charges. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arsuf
- Mikatagahara: rally and counter-raid were by night; Ieyasu's rally was hours (battle in the afternoon, the raid that night). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Mikatagahara
- Edgehill: Royalist cavalry returned from Kineton "late in the fight" (an hour or more). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Edgehill
- Lewes: Edward's return from a 6 km pursuit came after the king's wing was already beaten. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Lewes

Rough fractions (design estimates, not sourced figures):

- Fraction of a broken force that re-entered the same battle: usually small. In the cases where anything rallied it was a few hundred out of thousands (Arsuf's 700; Edgehill's 200; Mikatagahara a handful at first, then a night raid by about 100 foot and 16 gunners). Say **5 to 15 percent** of routed men for a typical battle, near **0 to 3 percent** for levy foot in open country with a hot pursuit, and **20 to 40 percent** for elite household cavalry with a commander alive and no pursuit.
- Combat effectiveness: Ayyubid rallied bodyguards hit once and broke again against formed charging cavalry (Arsuf). Hospitaller/English rallied troops were useful when used as a counter-stroke on disordered pursuers (Hastings), not for another set-piece fight. Mikatagahara raid was a small harassing night attack, not a pitched battle. Conclusion: rallied troops are fit for opportunistic counter-attacks, hold-the-line, or covering a retreat, and should not close with fresh formed troops without a morale penalty.

## 6. Pursuit discipline

### 6.1 Victors who dispersed

- **Lewes 1264:** Edward's cavalry broke Segrave's Londoners and "recklessly pursued" them about 4 miles (6 km); the King was left unsupported; the barons' reserve beat Henry's own attack; Edward returned with exhausted men to a lost battle. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Lewes
- **Edgehill 1642:** Rupert's and Wilmot's cavalry chased to Kineton and looted the baggage; Parliament's reserve then routed the Royalist foot. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Edgehill
- **Hastings 1066:** the English pursued down the hill and were cut off; the later feigned flights exploited the same urge. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Hastings
- **Arsuf 1191:** the Hospitallers' early break and Richard's three-stage recall are the counter-example: halting a pursuit after 1.5 km kept the army in hand. Some knights who ran too far were killed. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arsuf
- **Agincourt:** the French horse routed from the English stakes and trampled their own men; the English men-at-arms then moved forward and killed or captured the French in melee instead of pursuing across the field. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Agincourt
- **Nagashino:** Nobunaga's pursuit of broken Takeda was effective because the pursuers were in hand and the retreat was already cut. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Nagashino

### 6.2 Feigned flight as a deliberate tool

Sun Tzu advised "Do not pursue an enemy who simulates flight." The Rout article also notes a feigned rout can turn into a real one. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rout . Hastings is the medieval example. Arsuf shows Muslim light cavalry turning on over-extended knights.

### 6.3 Templar rules against breaking ranks

I could not retrieve the verbatim text **[unverified]**. The secondary accounts (Baucent article) record only the rules about the banner and leaving the field. The Arsuf account shows the Hospitaller marshal breaking the six-trumpet-blast order, which is evidence that the rule existed in practice (Garnier asked leave and was refused) and was broken under pressure. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Arsuf

## 7. Wargame rules for comparison

- **Lion Rampant (2nd ed.):** A failed Courage test makes a unit either rout off the table or become Battered ("useless until they rally"), depending on the margin of failure. A rally phase lets units that failed a Courage test the previous turn try to rally; failed rally attempts do not end the activation. Tests are triggered by casualties, a leader killed or fleeing, and when the army has lost half its units. https://www.historyofwar.org/bookpage/mersey_lion_rampant.html ; https://ospreypublishing.com/us/osprey-blog/2022/lion-rampant-second-edition-getting-started/
- **Hail Caesar:** Stamina-based. Casualties accumulate to the Stamina value, then the unit is Shaken, and Shaken units cannot charge. (6 for standard units, 4 for small.) I did not retrieve the rally procedure. https://www.tapatalk.com/groups/theoutpost/hail-dux-t225.html
- **DBA/DBM, Total War:** searches returned nothing usable; no claim is made here. **[unverified]**
- Pattern across rule sets worth copying: a graded state (Shaken/Battered/Routed), a test, a leader modifier, and a half-army collapse trigger.

## 8. Game rules suggested

All numbers are tunable design estimates built on the cases above. Distances assume 1 tile about 50 m; adjust to the game scale.

### 8.1 States

1. **Wavering** (shaken): cannot charge, -morale, may hold. Entered after heavy loss or a flank hit. Matches Hail Caesar "Shaken" and Lion Rampant "Battered".
2. **Routing**: moves directly away from nearest enemy and toward the nearest friendly strongpoint (camp, reserve, castle gate, standard), drops out of combat, does not attack. Pushes through and disorders friendly units it passes (Crécy, Agincourt, Edgehill).
3. **Scattered**: routing for more than the rally window or past the distance limit; removed from the battle (counts as a casualty for score, not dead). Matches Lewes at 6 km and Bannockburn's lost levies.
4. **Rallied (Shaken)**: back under orders with penalties below.

### 8.2 Triggers (make rout start and spread)

- Unit morale below threshold from: casualties (about 25 to 35 percent for levies, 40 to 50 percent for elites), flank or rear attack, own commander killed or fleeing, standard lost.
- **Contagion:** when a unit within about 100 m routs, each friendly unit tests; levy +risk, elite -risk. Rear-rank/idle units are more at risk than engaged ones.
- **Army collapse:** if 50 percent of units have routed, the rest test with a big penalty (Lion Rampant).
- **Commander rumour:** if the general is killed or out of sight for more than a short window (Hastings), nearby units get a rout check; the general re-appearing within the window (about 1 min) cancels it for those units.
- **Defection** (for Samurai scenarios): an optional faction event as at Sekigahara.

### 8.3 Who can rally and when

Rally check each short interval (about 5 to 10 seconds of game time) while a routing unit is inside a rally window and range.

- **Window:** 60 to 120 s of game time for elite/household units, 30 to 60 s for trained professionals, 15 to 30 s for levies. After the window or beyond the **distance cap** (cavalry about 1.5 to 2 km, foot about 0.5 to 1 km), the unit is Scattered.
- **Preconditions:** no enemy within about 100 m (out of contact), a rally anchor available, and the unit has not already rallied once (second rout = no rally).
- **Rally anchors:**
  - Own standard (banner / uma-jirushi) held by a living standard-bearer: strongest.
  - Commander in person within about 150 m: strong (Hastings, Arsuf, Mikatagahara).
  - Reserve (unrouted formed unit of the same side with an intact banner) within about 300 m: medium.
  - Fortified place (camp, castle gate) within range: medium, and a fugitive that reaches it becomes Rallied after a delay (Hamamatsu).
  - Wagon laager/baggage guard: medium for foot, a trap if the enemy wins the baggage (Agincourt).

### 8.4 Odds by troop quality (probability per check, before modifiers)

| Troops | Base | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Elite household cavalry/knights, samurai retinue, Templar brothers | 0.35 | Arsuf bodyguards; Hattin knights fight on |
| Veteran professionals, mercenaries (light) | 0.20 | |
| Trained spearmen, dismounted knights | 0.15 | Berkeley's Welsh |
| Levy foot, archers, crossbow mercs, undead rabble | 0.05 | Hattin infantry, Genoese |

Modifiers: +0.20 commander in person; +0.15 own standard alive and visible; +0.10 reserve/fort in range; +0.10 on terrain with a hill/wood within 100 m; -0.20 enemy cavalry within pursuit range; -0.15 commander dead; -0.15 own standard lost; -0.10 per 10 minutes of battle fatigue; -0.20 if encircled or without water (Hattin).

Expected outcome: about 5 to 15 percent of all routed men in a typical battle return to the field, 20 to 40 percent of elite cavalry with commander alive and no pursuit, about 0 to 3 percent of levy foot in the open with cavalry on their heels.

### 8.5 State after rallying

- Rallied (Shaken) for 30 to 60 s: cannot charge a fresh formed unit; -25 percent attack and morale; first loss re-routs them automatically and permanently (no second rally).
- May: hold a position, cover a retreat, counter-strike disordered or pursuing enemies at full morale (Hastings counter, Mikatagahara raid), or follow orders to fall back.
- Strength regained is only the men who actually returned. Do not heal losses.
- Recovery to normal morale after 3 to 5 minutes out of combat with the banner standing.

### 8.6 Joining another unit

- Default (all factions): fugitives rally only on their own banner or commander. If their banner is gone but a same-class unit (cavalry with cavalry, foot with foot) of the same army is rallying within about 150 m, they may attach at half the odds and arrive as a smaller, lower-morale element (Edgehill ad hoc groups).
- Cross-class joining: not allowed (no foot joining cavalry).
- **Templar trait ("Beauseant rule"):** a Templar unit separated from its banner automatically heads to the nearest friendly banner (any Christian banner, including Hospitallers; attached units take the banner's orders) and gains +0.20 rally chance within 200 m of any banner; Templars cannot voluntarily rout or leave the field while at least one order banner is still raised (use as "stand fast": they do not enter Routing unless the banner is gone, but they take a morale penalty and cannot retreat on player order). Penalty, as in the history: a unit that stands is wiped out by encirclement (Hattin). If all banners are lost, they become Routing and may join the nearest Hospitaller or friendly banner. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baucent, citing Schottmuller via Munter. Verify against Upton-Ward before shipping flavour text.
- **Samurai trait:** a very strong rally on own lord's uma-jirushi (+0.20) but no cross-clan joining unless the lord is a vassal of the same overlord; loss of lord = automatic dissolution (Otani, Ukita). Optional "advance-retreat" sacrifice: a rear unit may hold to let others escape (Natsume at Mikatagahara, Shimazu at Sekigahara).
- **Undead trait:** invent as a design choice (not a historical claim). Suggest: no morale and no rout, but cohesion tied to the necromancer: when the leader dies, all undead within range collapse, matching the historical pattern that loss of the commander ends cohesion.

### 8.7 Pursuit discipline

- Victorious units that are chasing (cavalry, light troops) enter **Pursuit** state: faster, +attack against routing, but no cohesion bonus and they do not respond to commander orders for the first N seconds unless the commander is within about 100 m.
- Each pursuing unit tests each interval; failure = **Carried away** beyond a limit (about 1.5 km) and becomes Dispersed for 60 to 180 s (looting baggage if in range; Edgehill, Lewes). Disciplined units (Templars with marshal alive, Samurai with a strong general) get +0.20.
- Pursuing units attacked in flank by a Rallied unit or a reserve take a morale penalty (Hastings, Arsuf, Lewes, Edgehill).
- A "recall" order costs a short delay; recall is easier within 1.5 km (Richard at Arsuf).
- Pursuit kills: routing units caught by cavalry take most of the casualties; target 0.15 to 0.30 of strength lost per minute in contact, tuned so pursuit accounts for the majority of the loser's total dead (Bannockburn and Nagashino).
